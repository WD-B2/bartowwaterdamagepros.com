Selling a House With Water Damage in Bartow, FL: What to Disclose

Water damage does not end a sale, but hiding it can. This guide explains the Florida rule, which records help and what to ask a professional.

By the Bartow Water Damage Pros team

24/7 emergency line(844) 950-6900Call now

The Florida rule in one paragraph

Florida does not rely on a single state-mandated seller form. The duty to disclose comes from a 1985 Florida Supreme Court case, Johnson v. Davis. A seller who knows of facts that materially affect the value of the property, and that the buyer cannot easily see, must disclose them.

Water damage fits that description when it is hidden, recurring or affects value. A leaking roof, past flooding or water intrusion are the typical examples. This is general information and not legal advice. A Florida real estate attorney can tell you how it applies to your sale.

What is generally treated as material

  • A roof leak that was repaired, or one that is still active.
  • Past flooding of the house, even if it has dried.
  • Plumbing leaks that damaged walls, floors or ceilings.
  • Mold that followed a water loss, and what was done about it.
  • Drainage problems that put water against the house.

A repaired problem still usually needs to be mentioned. The difference is that a repair with documentation looks very different from an unknown history. Buyers and their inspectors find a great deal, and disclosure sheds the doubt.

Radon is a separate written disclosure

Florida law requires a specific written disclosure about radon gas in connection with the sale of a building. It is a standard notice, and it applies regardless of testing. It is unrelated to water damage, but it sits in the same file of required paperwork, so ask your agent to include it.

Records that make a sale easier

Invoices and scope

Paperwork from the company that dried and repaired the building.

Moisture readings

Dated readings that show the structure reached normal levels.

Photos

Before and after pictures with dates, stored together.

Permits

Permit numbers and inspection sign-offs for plumbing, electrical or structural work.

Clearance report

If mold was involved, the assessor report that confirms the area passed.

A folder of these documents turns a worrying history into a documented one. Buyers can verify that the problem was handled, and that is often what they want to know.

Fix before listing, or sell as is

If the damage is current, the choice is between fixing it before you list and selling as is. Fixing costs money and time but can widen the buyer pool, since a house with an active water problem is harder for some buyers to finance or insure. Selling as is may be the right choice for a seller who cannot afford the work, but the disclosure duty still applies.

An as-is contract changes who pays for repairs. Florida courts have held that it does not erase the seller duty to disclose known, material, hidden defects. In either case, stopping the water and drying the structure first protects the value that is left. See structural drying and dehumidification for how drying is documented.

Flood history deserves extra care

If your Bartow home took water in a recent storm

East Bartow has flooded from the Peace River. After Hurricane Ian in 2022 the river stayed high for weeks, and Tropical Storm Debby in August 2024 brought it to 7.6 feet and prompted an emergency declaration. If your house took water in one of these events, the buyer will want to know.

Check the FEMA Flood Map Service Center for your flood zone. A flood insurance policy through the National Flood Insurance Program generally takes effect 30 days after purchase, which a buyer will take into account when planning insurance.

If the water came from outside, the cleanup is described on flood damage restoration. Nearby areas with their own flood histories are Fort Meade and Lakeland.

How buyers and inspectors look at water history

Inspectors check for staining, soft spots, high readings on a moisture meter and signs of repairs that do not match the rest of the room. A fresh patch of paint on a ceiling is a question mark, and they will ask about it. The kinder answer is a document that explains it.

Insurers may run a claims history on the property and ask about past water losses. Telling a buyer early, with records, is better than letting them find out at the inspection or at underwriting.

A step-by-step plan for a seller

  1. Gather every record from the water event: photos, readings, invoices, permits and reports.
  2. Write down the dates and what was done, in plain words, while you remember it.
  3. Talk to a Florida real estate attorney about what to disclose and how.
  4. Share the disclosure and the records with your listing agent.
  5. Be ready to answer questions from the buyer and the inspector with documents, not guesses.

Dealing with the buyer inspector

Inspectors look for the same signs a homeowner might miss. A moisture meter on a wall, a thermal camera on a ceiling and a look into the crawl space are routine. If your house has a history, the inspector will probably find the area.

Having the records on hand speeds the conversation. If the inspector finds something that your records do not explain, address it right away. Honesty and documents are the best defense if a buyer later has a question.

How water history affects negotiation

A documented, repaired problem usually leads to a short conversation. An undocumented one leads to questions about what else might be hidden. Buyers often ask for a price adjustment, a repair credit or a home warranty when they see signs they cannot explain. Records reduce those requests because they answer the question before it is asked.

If the damage is current and you cannot fix it before listing, be clear about what you know. Give the buyer the cause, the extent as far as you understand it and any estimates you have received. That openness protects you if a dispute comes up later.

Talk to the right people

Your listing agent knows the local forms and what buyers ask. A real estate attorney can advise on your duty to disclose. Your insurer can tell you whether a past claim will show on the property report. A restoration company can supply the records of the drying and cleanup.
