Sudden Water Bill Increase: How to Find a Hidden Leak in Bartow, FL

A high water bill with no change in habits is often the first sign of a leak. This guide shows a simple way to check, where leaks usually hide and who to ask about the bill.

By the Bartow Water Damage Pros team

Why a jump in the bill is worth chasing

Water bills rise with the season, with guests and with a filled pool. When none of those explain a jump, a leak is likely. Leaks can be small and constant, such as a toilet flapper that does not seal, or large and hidden, such as a supply line under a slab.

A leak that you can see is easy. A leak you cannot see can run for weeks and wet framing, insulation and flooring along the way. The bill is often the first signal.

A two-hour meter test

  1. 1

    Stop all use

    Make sure no one runs water, and that the washer, dishwasher and ice maker are not cycling.

  2. 2

    Read the meter

    Open the meter box and write down the number, or take a photo of it.

  3. 3

    Wait two hours

    Keep all water off for the whole time.

  4. 4

    Read again

    If the number moved, water is leaking somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

Many meters have a small leak indicator, a triangle or wheel that spins when any water is flowing. If it spins with everything off, the answer is the same. If the meter does not move, the leak may be on the drain side or the problem may be a billing issue to raise with the utility.

Common hidden leak locations

Toilets

A flapper that does not seal lets water trickle into the bowl all day. A drop of food coloring in the tank shows it if the color reaches the bowl without flushing.

Irrigation

Broken sprinkler heads and buried lines run when no one is watching.

Water heater

A tank leak or relief valve drip can run for a long time before it shows.

Under the slab

A pressurized line under the floor can leak into soil, and signs include warm spots, damp flooring and the meter moving.

Pool fill

An auto-fill valve can top up a leaking pool or a pool that is losing water.

If you think the leak is under the floor, see slab leak water damage for what that involves. A water heater that is dripping is covered on water heater leak cleanup.

Who to call about the bill

Water and sewer in the City of Bartow are provided by the city, which owns the utilities. If you live elsewhere, your provider may be a different city, a county utility or a private company. Your bill names the provider and gives a customer service number.

Ask whether the utility offers any adjustment for a leak that you have since repaired. Policies differ. Some adjust the sewer portion of the bill, some adjust nothing. Ask early, keep the plumber receipt and take photos of the repair.

A neighboring example

Eagle Lake sewer credit rules

The City of Eagle Lake offers a sewer credit for water service leaks and pool fills. A customer has to request it within 30 days and provide plumber receipts and photos. The credit applies to sewer charges, and water usage is not adjusted.

That kind of rule shows why timing matters. If you wait months to ask, you may miss the window. Whatever city you live in, ask the utility for its deadline the day you fix the leak.

If the leak has already wet the building

Finding a leak stops the water. It does not dry what the water touched. If it ran for weeks, there may be wet drywall, damp insulation, swollen flooring or a musty smell. Moisture readings show how far it went.

The EPA recommends drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, so once the leak is fixed, the clock for the damage that is already there starts. Documentation helps with insurance. The Florida Department of Financial Services advises photographing the damage before temporary repairs and contacting your insurer before you sign a repair contract. Drying follows the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard.

Cost of waiting

A small leak left alone grows into damage that is more expensive to fix than the leak itself. Wet subfloors swell, drywall softens and mold can follow when materials stay damp. The bill increase is a nuisance. The damage is what changes the scope of the repair.

There is also the question of the claim. Gradual leaks that go unfixed for a long time are often treated differently from sudden ones, which is a reason to move quickly and document what you find.

Reading the bill itself

Look at the usage line, not just the dollar total. Compare it with the same month last year, because summer use is usually higher. Check the service dates, since a longer billing period means a higher number. If the usage is much higher with no change in your habits, you have a real signal.

Ask the utility if the meter reading was actual or estimated. An estimate that caught up after a correction can look like a spike. The customer service line can also tell you whether other customers on your street have reported a main issue.

  • Same month last year versus this month.
  • Number of days in the billing period.
  • Whether the reading was actual or estimated.
  • Any change in irrigation, pool or guests.

Irrigation and pools: the leaks people forget

Sprinkler systems are a frequent source of unseen water use. A cracked head or a stuck valve can run for hours. Walk the yard after the system has run and look for pooling, soggy ground or unusually green patches.

Pools lose water to evaporation and to leaks. A simple bucket test, comparing the water level inside and outside the pool over a day, helps separate the two. If you have an automatic fill valve, check whether it is running more than it should.

What to do this week

  1. Run the two-hour meter test.
  2. Check every toilet and every visible supply line.
  3. Look at the water heater and the area around it.
  4. If the meter still moves, call a plumber with leak detection equipment.
  5. Photograph any damage, then call (844) 950-6900 if materials are wet.
